1. Introduction

There are two types of motion which play especially important roles in classical as well as quantum) mechanics - harmonic motion and central-force motion. Harmonic motion was covered in units 5 and 6 using Newtons laws of motion. Central-force motion has been reserved for this unit to illustrate the use of the Lagrangian formulation. All of the results of this unit can be obtained, of course, without recourse to the Lagrangian formulation. In fact you may have obtained them in a previous mechanics course. If that is the case, this unit will serve as a review of central-force motion as well as an example of the application of the Lagrangian technique.

3. Conic Sections

A1l conic sections can be represented by the single mathematical expression

\[\frac{\alpha}{r} = 1 + \epsilon \cos \theta\,,\]

where \(r\) and \(\theta\) are radial and angular variables respectively while \(\epsilon\) and \(\alpha\) are constants:

\[\epsilon = \text{ eccentricity},\]

\[\alpha = \text{ semi-latus rectum}.\]

The name conic section comes from the observation that these curves are precisely those obtained as the perimeters of slices (or sections) through a right circular cone.

  1. 1. ellipse - the locus of points for which the sum of the distances from two fixed points (\(F\), \(F'\) = foci) is a constant (see Fig. 1).

    By definition for any point \(P\) on the ellipse:

    \[r + r' = 2 Q = \text{ constant}.\]

    Inspection of the figure then shows that

    \[OV = FB = a = \text{ semimajor axis},\]

    and

    \[VV' = 2 a = \text{ major axis } = \text{ intervertical distance}.\]

    For convenience let:

    \[b = O B = \text{ semiminor axis},\]

    \[\epsilon = \text{ interfocal distance}.\]

    Then inspection of the figure again shows that

    \[OF = a \epsilon\,,\]

    \[b = a \sqrt{1 - \epsilon^2}\,.\]

    To determine the equation of the ellipse note that the definition gives:

    \[(r')^2 = (2a - r)^2 = 4 a^2 - 4ar + r^2\,,\]

    while the law of cosines gives

    \[(r')^2 = r^2 + 4 a^2 \epsilon^2 - 2(2 a \epsilon) r \cos (\pi - \theta)\,,\]

    \[= r^2 + 4 a^2 \epsilon^2 + 4 a \epsilon r \cos \theta\,.\]

    Equating these and rearranging gives

    \[\frac{a (1 - \epsilon^2)}{r} = 1 + \epsilon \cos \theta\,.\]

    This is the equation of a conic section with

    \[\epsilon = \text{ eccentricity}\]

    (justifying the earlier choice of notation)

    \[\alpha = a (1 - \epsilon^2) = \text{ semi-latus rectum}\,.\]

    Letting \(\theta = \pi / 2\) in the equation of the ellipse and consulting the figure reveals that

    \[FA = \alpha\,.\]

  2. 2. parabola - the locus of points equidistant from a straight line (\(D\) = directrix) and a point (\(F\) = focus) (see Fig. 2).

    For convenience let

    \[\frac{a}{2} = FV = VO\,.\]

    Then from the figure for any point \(P\) on the parabola

    \[r + r \cos \theta = a\,,\]

    so that

    \[\frac{a}{r} = 1 + \cos \theta\,.\]

    This is the equation of a conic section with an eccentricity of 1 and with

    \[\alpha = a = \text{ semi-latus rectum}.\]

    Letting \(\theta = - \pi / 2\), shows that

    \[FA = \alpha\,.\]

  3. 3. hyperbola - the locus of points for which the difference between the distances from two fixed points (\(F\), \(F'\) = foci) is a constant (see Fig. 3).

    By definition for any point \(P\) on the hyperbola

    \[r - r' = 2 a = \text{ constant}.\]

    Inspection of the figure then shows that

    \[OV = a\,.\]

    As in the case of the ellipse it is convenient to define

    \[\epsilon = \frac{\text{interfocal distance}}{\text{intervertical distance}}\,.\]

    The definition of a hyperbola then gives

    \[(r')^2 = (2 a + r)^2 = 4 a^2 + 4ar + r^2\,,\]

    which with the law of cosines gives

    \[(r')^2 = r^2 + 4 a^2 \epsilon^2 - 4(4 a \epsilon) r \cos \theta\,.\]

    Equating and rearranging yields

    \[\frac{a (\epsilon^2 - 1)}{r} = 1 + \epsilon \cos \theta\,.\]
