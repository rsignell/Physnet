A. Introduction

The Universal Law of Gravitation describes a force between masses. Any work done in changing a configuration of masses thus results in a change in potential energy. The formula for this energy is derived and used, particularly in problems involving conservation of energy.

D. Exercises

  1. 1. Articulate why one uses conservation of energy in the assigned problems rather than, say, getting the velocities from \(v = v_0 + at\).
  2. 2. Derive Newton's Law of Gravitation from the expression for gravitational potential energy by applying the gradient operator. For you skiers, the gradient of a function points up the function's fall line.
  3. Note that, in two dimensions, the gradient operator is: \[\Grad \equiv \text{\bf grad} \equiv \hat{x}\partiald{x} + \hat{y}\partiald{y} = \hat{r} \partiald{r} + \hat{\theta} \frac{1}{r} \partiald{\theta}\,.\]
  4. Now: \[\partial E_p(r)/\partial \theta = 0,\]
  5. so: \[{\Grad E_p(r) \equiv \text{\bf grad} } E_p(r) = \frac{dE_P(r)}{dr}\, \hat{r}\,.\]

Problem Supplement

solar.dat

Work the AF problems listed in this module's text.

  1. 1. Start from Newton's Law of Gravitation and derive the general expression for gravitational potential energy.
  2. 2. A projectile is fired vertically from the earth toward the moon.
  3. a. At what point on its path toward the moon will its acceleration be zero?
  4. b. What would the minimum muzzle velocity (initial velocity of the projectile) need to be to allow the projectile to reach that point, and then fall toward the moon under the influence of the moon's gravity. Assume air resistance is negligible.
  5. c. In this case, what would the projectile's velocity be when it hit the moon?
  6. 3. Reduce the general expression for gravitational potential energy to the earth's surface formula \(E_p = m g h\).

Brief Answers

  1. 1. \(F = - \frac{G m m'}{r^2}\,\hat{r} \hs\) (Newton's Law of Gravitation).
  2. Designating (\(r = \infty\)) as the potential energy reference point, \(E_p(\infty) = 0\), and denoting the radial component of \(\vec{F}\) as \(F_r\):
  3. \(E_p(r) = - \int_{\infty}^r \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = - \int_{\infty}^r F_r\, dr = \int_{\infty}^r \frac{G m m'}{r^2} \,dr\)
  4. \(\phantom{E_p(r)} = G m m' \int_{\infty}^r r^{-2}\,dr = - \frac{G m m'}{r}\,.\)
  5. 2. a. \(x = \frac{d_{ME}}{1 + \sqrt{m_M/m_E}}\).
  6. b. \(V_0 = \sqrt{ 2 G \left( \frac{m_E}{R_E} + \frac{m_M}{d_{ME} - R_E} - \frac{m_E}{x} - \frac{m_M}{d_{ME} - x} \right)}\).
  7. c. \(v_f = \sqrt{ 2 G \left( \frac{m_E}{d_{ME} - R_M} + \frac{m_M}{R_M} - \frac{m_E}{x} - \frac{m_M}{d_{ME} - x} \right)}\).
  8. NOTE: Some of the above terms are negligibly small.
  9. 3. \(\Delta E_p = E_p (R_E + h) - E_p(R_E) = -\frac{G m m_E}{R_E + h} + \frac{G m m_E}{R_E} = \frac{G m m_E h}{R_E (R_E + h)}\)
  10. At earth's surface:
  11. \(F = mg = \frac{G m m_E}{R_E^2}\), so: \(G m_E = g R_E^2\)
  12. Putting this into the above,
  13. \(\Delta E_p = \frac{m g h R_E}{R_E + h} = m g h \frac{1}{1 + h/R_E}\)
  14. Since the quantity in the parentheses will be negligibly changed by exclusion of \((h/R_E)\), providing (\(h \ll R_E\)), we get:
  15. \(\Delta E_p \approx m g h, \qquad h \ll R_E\).
  16. If we change our potential energy reference:
  17. \(E_p(h) \approx m g h, \qquad h \ll R_E\).
