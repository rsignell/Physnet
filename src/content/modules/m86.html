1. Authoring a Modular Lesson

1a. The Modular Lesson: Description

A modular lesson performs a definite instructional function which is described by well-articulated input and output statements. The input statements specify the skills and knowledge which are prerequisite to the lesson, while the output statements describe the skills and knowledge which are developed in the lesson. It is the job of an editor to try to fill gaps in the network. Such modular lessons can be aimed at bringing into the curriculum exciting new scientific developments and viewpoints, dramatically improved pedagogy, etc. The author of a lesson can be any scientist-teacher who is willing to submit a properly formatted manuscript for review and publication. The requirements are entirely similar to those traditionally specified by good scholarly journals.

1b. Module Length: The “Unit”

A modular lesson can be as short as just a few minutes of learning time, or as long as a semester, but the basic traditional modular lesson, the lecture, is about one hour long. Since there is some indication that even this may be a little on the long side, we take the basic modular lesson size, the “unit,” as no more than lecture-length. If an author wishes to construct a longer sequence of instruction, we suggest constructing an overview unit and set of accompanying lecture-length units.

1c. Lesson Development: Author Options

The usual goal in authoring a modular lesson is the creation of an advance in instructional materials. This might be a pedagogical advance over existing materials or an expository advance which brings in useful new skills and knowledge. For example, a pedagogical advance over standard textbook materials might be achieved through improved design and testing strategies. On the other hand, an expository advance might be achieved by making available exciting new topics and skills. Lessons which accomplish this sometimes have less than optimum instructional designs: an editor may even have to articulate the author's lesson goals and prerequisites. However, a good teacher can use careful design and testing to convert another author's expository lesson into an excellent pedagogical one, as illustrated in the figure.

1d. Lesson Function: Author Options

A modular lesson can usually be classified as orientation, a cognitive map, instruction, or a task algorithm.

i) An orientation-type module presents an introduction to, and an overview of, knowledge and skills which are further developed in instruction-type lessons. An orientation module is designed both to help the reader articulate needs and locate relevant materials, and to provide a cognitive framework, or overview, for subsequent detailed study.

ii) A map-type module presents a new and unique cognitive organization for those already familiar with an area. It contains no introductory material and is usually concerned with reorganizing and area having recent high research activity.

iii) An instruction-type lesson represents the ultimate material for the instructional process: it provides imaginative, authoritative instruction in skills and knowledge needed to solve present and future real-life problems in the pure and applied sciences. It generally contains an orientation section with both introductory and overview functions.

4. A task algorithm sets out eh bare-bones steps needed to accomplish a task. It is always preferred over instruction for cases where a complex task is to be carried out infrequently. In addition, some researchers believe that almost all learning is algorithmic, and that a high level of understanding is merely evidence of a number of highly useful algorithms.

1e. Research-Guided Lesson Design

Modular lesson guidelines are based upon published research in the fields of communication, linguistics, and psychology.

See \Quote{Ideas for Designing Instructional Modules} (MISN-6-136) for additional description and for the actual literature references.For example, research has shown, time-and-again, the efficacy of starting a topic with a general overview, then following that overview with examples. The characteristics of both overview and examples have been varied and optimal design characteristics developed. Such characteristics usually turn out to be in close agreement with techniques long used by successful writers and advocated by good writing teachers. In addition, such communication-research findings and their relation to results of cognition research provide a deeper understanding of the techniques and new impetus to make use of them.

1f. Goals for Use

Modular lesson design is intended to be compatible with a wide variety of instructional delivery modes. The modular materials should help instructors in lecture design and exam generation. They should be usable as textbook supplements or to form entire custom textbooks. In fact, traditional textbooks can be comparison rated using the modules' design criteria. Where it is judged desirable, they should facilitate the development of independent learning skills. They should easily adapt to various student backgrounds, no matter how diverse. They should allow teachers to make completely credible the network of prerequisites needed by the learner. They should allow a practicing professional to easily identify, locate and acquire needed skills and knowledge. Most of these goals require highly flexible materials with well-articulated goals and strategies. By incorporating such features into the lesson design, the resulting materials should allow the rise of new and innovative delivery systems. Whether specific systems die or evolve, the modular lesson system should continue its development as a support system for all of them.

2. Structural Design Features

2a. Applying Psycholinguistic Research

The structure and organization of a module can be designed to enhance storage in the learner's memory, and subsequently to enhance retrieval from it. Numerous experiments in psychology and linguistics have been aimed at determining those characteristics which tend to optimize storage and retrieval. For example, in optimizing paragraph structure for storage and retrieval, it has been found that a properly constructed lead-off topic sentence is more efficient than a similarly placed topic question. However, both were found to be more efficient than evidence-to-conclusion structures. Other experiments and their interpretation apply to many facets of lesson structure, both for promoting storage and retrieval and for the further strengthening of retrievability.

2b. Structure to Promote Storage and Retrieval

In order to enhance comprehension and memory, a module should be organized into a cohesive hierarchical structure. Such a structured module typically: 1. has opening overviews at all levels; 2. has a very limited number of smaller chunks in each larger chunk;

A chunk consists of bits of information which have been mentally combined for the purpose of comprehension. For more information see \Quote{Stalking the Elusive Chunk} (MISN-6-60).3. goes from general to specific material; and 4. gives immediate concrete support to abstract ideas. To assist the mental categorization necessary for storage and recall, the module can include a Table of Contents containing section and sub-section headings arranged in outline style.

For an example, see this module's Table of Contents.To provide more detailed categorization, each lesson can start with an overview section, each section with an overview paragraph, and each paragraph with an overview topic sentence. There should be no more than about four or five content chunks in any one grouping, if one wishes to avoid overloading short-term memory.

Short-term memory is where information is held while being organized and coded for longer term storage. For more information see \Quote{Stalking the Elusive Chunk} (MISN-6-60).This implies a maximum of about five non-overview sections per lecture-length lesson and a similar maximum of non-overview paragraphs per section. For similar reasons, and also because of the memory's preferred recall of first elements, the material should preferably be arranged from the general to the specific. Finally, abstract ideas should be given immediate concrete support, especially in the form of illustrative examples and graphics, which help the reader to decide which phenomena are inside the concept's region of applicability and which are outside it.

2c. Retrievals to Strengthen Retrievability

Each retrieval from memory strengthens retrieval paths for future recall, so a lesson should be designed to promote recalls. For example, retrieval of previously learned material can be prompted by providing the minimum help necessary for the learner. Thus learner-suppliable steps can be omitted from lines of argument and from derivations, and reviews avoided. At points where some readers are likely to become stuck, references can be inserted which point to prior lessons for prerequisite skills and to a Special Assistance Supplement for tutorial assistance. Another method of enhancing retrievals of lesson material is that of making the Table of Contents, an Index, and the Skills Statements suitable for learner self-testing after completion of the module. Finally, appropriate examples can help promote retrievals in the learner's daily life, thus greatly strengthening long-term retrievability.

2d. Graduated Help to Assist Retrievability

Since a lesson's main presentation is generally constructed with gaps which are filled in by most learners, special assistance needs to be provided for those learners who cannot fill them. This assistance can be provided in supplements which are printed separately but are a part of the lesson package. Such supplements are designed to 1. save learners' time by obviating the necessity to come to a staff area; 2. save staff time by eliminating repetitious assistance; and 3. provide learners with controlled assistance. Learners are branched to such supplements from main presentations and from applications problems via Special-Assistance-Sequence (SAS) reference numbers. An SAS is a sort of tutorial which provides graduated assistance in the form of diagnoses, hints, suggestions, questions, and references to prerequisites which went unacquired. The SAS often appears as programmed instruction, containing many branches, graphics, and skills tests. However, it should seek to improve the learner's general learning and problem-solving skills so as to reduce its own necessity. Generally, a lesson author designs an initial set of SAS's, based upon personal teaching experience. Other necessary SAS's are identified through well-defined field testing.

3. Presentation Techniques

3a. Techniques for Specific Purposes

It is generally useful for an author to be aware of the cognitive utility of various presentation techniques, so as to be able to utilize those techniques in appropriate places and with optimal effect. For example, specific techniques can be used to hold the reader's attention, which is necessary for the transfer of information form short- to long-term memory. Graphics can be used to strengthen concept storage and retrieval by linking the powerful pattern memory to verbal memory. Direct learner involvement with the material can be encouraged by specific writing techniques and by the communication of a well-designed attitude toward the reader. Such techniques can also foster learner independence from excessive assistance by staff and materials.

3b. Promoting Storage Via Attention

Attention is a necessity for the transfer of information from short- to long-term memory, so a good module is designed to hold the reader's interest. One way of encouraging attention is to use striking examples and graphics. Another way is to involve the reader through hypothesis-forming overviews and interesting in-the-text applications problems which the reader can solve. In this regard, one can consciously avoid making remarks which damage the reader's sense of being a conscientious learner. In any case, such remarks do not apply to all readers, and hence can be relegated to the module's Special Assistance Supplement. A final technique is to include only cohesive material. Material included without a clear purpose is less likely to acquire the attention needed for transfer to long-term memory.

3c. Promoting Retrieval Via Pattern Memory

The brain's powerful capability for storing and retrieving patterns can be utilized in modules by effecting linkages between pattern memory and verbal memory. The simplest example is a line drawing which contains a clear pattern with a minimum of details, but also includes the relevant key concept words in the drawing itself or in its caption. For presenting a dynamic time-evolving pattern, poster graphics are useful, while a feeling of motion can usually be transmitted by means of the filmstrip technique.

Such techniques are described in some detail, with illustrations, in \Quote{Ideas for Constructing Instructional Modules} (MISN-6-136).Audio-visual materials ca also be used to accomplish such ends, but their advantages in specific applications must be judged to outweigh the disadvantages of their fixed presentation speed and the need for special equipment. Finally, it is sometimes feasible to have the learner construct graphics, such as well-labelled graphs and diagrams, thus combining the advantages of pattern-plus-verbal storage and learner involvement.

3d. Promoting Learner Independence

Moving the learner toward independence from minutely detailed guidance by staff and/or materials can help promote present instructional efficiency and the learner's future efficiency as a professional, as well as encourage originality and creativity. A favorable environment is created by an author's attitude of respect for the reader as a conscientious learner; evidenced, for example, by an absence of commands or imperative directions. One can demonstrate confidence in the reader's abilities by deliberately omitting review and reader-suppliable steps in arguments and derivations. For those readers who do not warrant such confidence, there is the Special Assistance Supplement. The labeling of such materials as “special assistance,” their separation from the main text, their graduated character, and their help in acquiring general learning and problem-solving skills
