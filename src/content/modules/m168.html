1. Introduction

1a. Classical vs. Quantum Distributions

While the Boltzmann distribution adequately describes the distribution of particle energies in many systems (such as gases at normal temperatures and pressures), it fails completely in systems where quantum effects are important. One very important case occurs in the study of the properties of metals. The so-called conduction electrons in a metal may be considered as a “gas” confined to the interior of the metal. Application of the Boltzmann distribution to this gas results in incorrect conclusions concerning the contributions of the electrons to the specific heat and magnetic susceptibility of the metal. In such cases it is necessary to use the correct quantum statistical distribution.

1b. Quantum Distributions: Fermi-Dirac and Bose-Einstein

There are two different quantum distributions since there are two fundamentally different types of particles--fermions, which obey the Pauli exclusion principlet, and bosons, which are not restricted by the exclusion principle. Fermions are particles which have half-odd integer spin such as electrons, protons, neutrons, and some nuclei. These particles have a Fermi-Dirac energy distribution. Bosons are particles with zero or integer spin such as photons, pions, and some nuclei. These particles have a Bose-Einstein energy distribution. In situations where classical physics provides an adequate description, the Boltzmann distribution is a close approximation to both the Fermi-Dirac and Bose-Einstein distributions.

No two fermions can occupy exactly the same quantum state.

1c. Distribution Among Energy Levels

The distribution of particles among energy levels is described by giving the occupation numbers \(N_i\), the number of particles having energy \(E_i\). The index \(i\) ranges over a set of values (integers, for example) which distinguish the possible energies which the particles can have. There are two important quantities which are related to the distribution. One, the Fermi Energy �F is the energy of the highest occupied level at absolute zero temperature. The other quantity is the chemical potential  which is equal to the Fermi Energy at absolute zero but decreases as temperature increases, eventually becoming negative. We discover that as the temperature increases or density decreases, the limit of the Fermi Distribution is the Boltzmann Distribution.

The chemical potential appears as a normalization constant in our discussion, but it is an important quantity in thermodynamics and statistical mechanics where it appears as the quantity which determines the equilibrium concentration of reactants in chemical reactions. The term \Quote{chemical} is to be interpreted in a very broad sense to include all sorts of reactions which proceed to an equilibrium state--nuclear and elementary particle reactions, transitions between various energy levels of atoms and molecules, transitions between states of ionization of atoms, to name just a few. For example, see the book by L.D. Landau and E.M. Lifshitz, \textit{Statistical Physics}, Addison-Wesley; Reading, MA (1958), Chapters 10 and 11.

1d. Distribution per Unit Energy Interval

By multiplying the number of particles in an energy level by the number of levels per unit energy interval we obtain the distribution of particles per unit energy interval. This allows us to replace sums over energy levels by integrals over a range of energies, a replacement which is generally a convenience. However this convenience involves an approximation: we are replacing a discrete set of energy levels with a continuous distribution, a number of levels per unit energy interval, called the density of states.

The approximation involved is much like describing a string by its linear mass density (i.e., mass per unit length) although we know that the string is really composed of discrete masses (molecules, atoms, elementary particles). In the case of the Fermi Energy Distribution the error involved is proportional to $1/N$ where $N$ is the total number of particles. For macroscopic systems $N$ is usually greater than about $10^{19}$.Armed with the density of states function and the energy distribution function we discover that the Fermi Energy is directly related to the number of particles per unit volume. We also determine the chemical potential as a function of temperature and density.

1e. Peculiar Properties of a Fermi Gas

When we apply the Fermi distribution to the problems of calculating the pressure and specific heat of a gas of fermions we discover a behavior at low temperatures and high densities which is quite unlike the properties pre- dicted by the Boltzmann distribution. While the specific heat of a Boltzmann gas is constant, the specific heat of the Fermi gas goes to zero as the temperature goes to zero. On the other hand, the pressure of a Boltzmann gas goes to zero as the temperature goes to zero, but the pressure of a Fermi gas goes to a constant value greater than zero. These peculiar properties are effects of the Pauli Exclusion Principle. Even at absolute zero all the particles cannot be in the lowest energy state but must fill all the lowest states sequentially. Thus the aggregate has a non-zero total energy and a non-zero pressure.

It is this so-called degenerate pressure of electrons which prevents the ultimate gravitational collapse of most stars which have used up all of their thermonuclear energy sources. Normally a star as massive as the sun is prevented from collapsing under the weight of its own gravitational forces by sources of heat energy which provide the pressure to keep it in equilibrium. When the energy sources are used up the star begins to cool and collapse. As the density in- creases, the electrons are ionized from the atoms and form an electron gas. If the electrons were not fermions the collapse would continue indefinitely as the star cooled. However because the electron gas becomes degenerate the pressure does not drop to zero and thus eventually supports the star against further collapse. The collapse is halted when the star has reached a size not much larger than the earth (the original volume was $10^6$ times the earth's volume) and the density of the material composing it is about \m{100,000\unit{g/cm\up{3}}}. Recently evidence of \Quote{dead} stars composed of a degenerate neutron gas has been discovered. These neutron stars are only about 10 miles in diameter and have densities of \m{10^{14}\unit{g/cm\up{3}}}.

2. Fermi-Dirac Statistics

2a. The Fermi-Dirac Distribution

The Fermi-Dirac distribution for a system of \(N\) non-interactingt fermions in thermal equilibrium at absolute temperature \(T\) is:

\[\mathrm{(F-D Distr.) } N_i = \frac{g_i}{e^{(\epsilon_i - \mu)/kT}+1}\]

The quantum-mechanically allowed energy levels of the fermions are denoted by \(\epsilon_i\) and the numbers \(g_i\) are the number of different quantum states corresponding to the level \(\epsilon_i\). (Example: Suppose \(\epsilon_1\), \(\epsilon_2\),..., etc. are the energy levels in a hydrogen atom. Then the corresponding \(g1\), \(g_2\),... etc. are \(g_1 = 2\), \(g_2 = 8\), \(g_3 = 18\),... [help 2]) Note that \(N_i\) is the average number of particles having energy \(\epsilon_i\), \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant (\(k = 1.38 \times 10^{23}JK^{-1})\), and \(\mu\) is a constant called the chemical potential which is determined by the requirement that all the \(N_i\) add up to \(N\), the total number of particles in the system:

\[N = \sum_i \frac{g_i}{e^{(\epsilon_i - \mu)/kT}+1}\]

Usually, \(N\) will be a very large number--for example, the number of valence electrons which constitute the conduction electron gas in sodium is \(2.5 \times 10^{28}\) per cubic meter. [help 1]

Non-interacting means that we neglect forces between particles such as gravitational or electrical. A gas of non-interacting particles is often called a \Quote{perfect gas}. In theory we must allow some interaction, in particular, occasional collisions between particles which allow the system to come to thermal equilibrium. If there were no interactions at all then a system not initially in an equilibrium state would never come to thermal equilibrium. For example, imagine that all the particles initially had the same velocity. Without random collisions the velocities would forever remain the same. The presence of random collisions, however, would eventually lead to an equilibrium distribution of velocities.

2b. Functional Dependence of the Chemical Potential

The chemical potential \(\mu\) depends in general on three things: (a) the composition of the system; (b) the number of particles per unit volume; and (c) the temperature. By composition of the system we mean such considerations as the properties of the particles composing the system (masses, spins, etc. of the particles) and the external forces applied to them.

For example, in the free electron theory of metals we imagine that the boundary of the metal constitutes a box within which the con- duction electrons are free to move. So we describe the forces on the electrons in this case by saying that as long as an electron is within the box, there is zero force on it but if it comes to the side of the box it feels a strong (infinite for an \Quote{ideal} box) repulsive force which prevents it from escaping from the box.Thus composition determines the energy levels \(\epsilon_i\) and the multiplicities \(g_i\) which appear in Eq. (2). We will see later that the right hand side of Eq. (2) is proportional to the volume \(V\) of the system therefore \(\mu\) depends on \(N/V\), the number of particles per unit volume. And finally, the dependence on temperature is obvious since the temperature occurs explicitly in Eq. (2).

2c. Occupation Probability: Effect of the Exclusion Principle

The Fermi-Dirac distribution given in Eq. ()1
illustrates the effect of the Pauli exclusion principle: no more than one fermion can occupy a given quantum state. Since there are \(g_i\) quantum states with energy \(\epsilon_i\), the ratio \(N_i/g_i\) is the average number of particles in one quantum state. Denote this ratio, the Fermi-Dirac Occupation Probability, \(P_i\):

\[(\mathrm{F-D Occ. Prob}.)\;\;P_i = \frac{N_i}{g_i} = \frac{1}{e^{\epsilon_i-\mu)/kT}+1}\]

But the denominator of Eq. (3) is always greater than one since the exponential is always positive. [help 12] Thus \(P_i\) is always a number between zero and one--there can never be more than one particle in a given quantum state. The number \(P_i\) can be thought of as the probability that the state is occupied. Fig. 1 shows a qualitative plot of \(P_i\) vs. \(\epsilon_i\) for some temperature greater than zero. Note that at the energy \(\epsilon_i = \mu\), the occupation probability is \(P_i = 1/2\).

2d. Absolute Zero Temperature: The Fermi Energy

At absolute zero temperature the \(N\) fermions occupy the \(N\) lowest energy quantum states and the states higher than the \(N\) lowest are empty.

We know that, for a gas with a Boltzmann distribution, the tempera- ture is proportional to the average energy of a particle of the gas (see U157, U159). Thus at zero temperature all of the particles should be in the lowest energy state (which is zero energy for free particles). In the case of fermions, the Pauli exclusion principle tells us that no more than one particle can occupy a given quantum state. Therefore the lowest average energy per particle is achieved when the $N$ lowest energy quantum states are filled sequentially.The energy level which corresponds to the highest filled state is called the Fermi level and the value of the energy is called the Fermi energy \(\epsilon_F\). As we will see, \(\epsilon_F\) is related only to the number density

of particles and the masses of the individual particles. We can verify that the states up to \(\epsilon_F\) but not beyond are filled and also that the value of \(\mu\) at \(T = 0\) is equal to the Fermi energy \(\epsilon_F\) by examining the expression for \(P_i\) given in Eq. (3)) in the limit as \(T \rightarrow 0\). [help 13]. Fig. 2 shows the graph of \(P_i\) vs \(\epsilon_2\) for \(T = 0\). A gas of fermions which is at absolute zero temperature is said to be “degenerate.”

3a. Energy Distribution Function

The energy distribution function \(f(\epsilon)\) is defined this way:

\(f(\epsilon)d\epsilon =\) number of particles per unit volume which have energies in the range \(\epsilon\) to \(\epsilon+d\epsilon\).

The Fermi-Dirac energy distribution function may be obtained from the Fermi-Dirac occupation probability by multiplying the occupation probability by the number of quantum states per unit volume per unit energy interval \(g(\epsilon)\):

\[f(\epsilon)d\epsilon = p(\epsilon) \times g(\epsilon)d\epsilon\]

where:

  • \(f(\epsilon)d\epsilon\) is the number of particles/volume in the energy range \(\epsilon\) to \(\epsilon+d\epsilon\),
  • \(p(\epsilon)\) is the probability of a particle occupying the state, and
  • \(g(\epsilon)d\epsilon\) is the number of states/volume in the energy range \(\epsilon\) to \(\epsilon+d\epsilon\).

3b. Relation Between the Fermi Energy and Number Density

The Fermi Energy \(\epsilon_F\) is proportional to the two-thirds power of the number density of particles \(n\), is inversely proportional to their masses \(m\), and also depends on the spin of the particles. For spin one-half particles the relation is:

\[\epsilon_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(3\pi^2n\right)^{2/3}\]

Eq. (6)) is derived by using the definition of \(\epsilon_F\). At absolute zero temperature all the energy states from \(\epsilon = 0\) to \(\epsilon = \epsilon_F\) are occupied so that the total number of particles \(n\) in a unit volume is just the total number of states between \(\epsilon = 0\) and \(\epsilon = \epsilon_F\). The total number of states is found by integrating the density of states from \(\epsilon = 0\) to \(\epsilon = \epsilon_F\). Thus

\[N = \int_{0}^{\epsilon_F} g(\epsilon) d\epsilon = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \int_0^{\epsilon_F}\sqrt{\epsilon}d\epsilon\]

Doing the integral in Eq. (7) we obtain Eq. (6).
