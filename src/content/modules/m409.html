[]BGravitational Force Near the Earth

As discussed in \TxtSectChRef6E, any particle falling near the earth under the sole influence of gravity has a downward acceleration \(\vex{g}\) which is independent of all properties of the particle. The value of this “gravitational acceleration” \(\vex{g}\) is nearly constant in any region of linear size much smaller than the radius of the earth (although it decreases slowly with increasing distance from the earth).

According to the definition of force, a particle of mass \(m\) moving with an acceleration \(\vex{a}\) is acted on by a force \(\vex{F} = m \vex{a}\). Since \(\vex{a} = \vex{g}\) for any particle subject only to the gravitational interaction with the earth, any such particle must then be acted on by a “gravitational force” \(\vex{F}_g\) due to the earth such that: *

\[\vex{F}_g = m \vex{g}\]

* Here and in the following sections we shall use a single subscript to indicate the interaction or object responsible for the force.
The properties of this gravitational force \(\vex{F}_g\) are then apparent from those of the gravitational acceleration \(\vex{g}\).
  1. (a) The gravitational force is a long-range force (since it acts on the particle even when this particle is at a considerable distance from the surface of the earth).
  2. (b) The gravitational force is attractive (since it is downward, i.e., toward the center of the earth).
  3. (c) The gravitational force depends on the mass of the particle but on none of its other properties (since \(g\) is independent of all properties of the particle).
  4. (d) The gravitational force is nearly constant within any region of linear size much smaller than the radius of the earth, but decreases slowly with increasing distance from the earth (since \(\vex{g}\) has the corresponding properties).

The word “weight” is commonly used in accordance with this definition:

Definition B2: Weight — The weight of an object is the magnitude of the gravitational force on this object.

Thus (B1) implies that the weight \(w\) of an object of mass \(m\) is simply \(w = |\vex{F}_g| = mg\). For example, near the surface of the earth where \(g = 10\) meter/sec\(^2\), the weight \(w\) of a newborn baby having a mass \(m = 4\) kg is \(mg =\)(4 kg)(10 meter/sec\(^2\)) = 40 newton. Note that the weight of an object, unlike its mass, depends not only on the properties of the object, but also on the location of the object relative to the earth which exerts the gravitational force. Thus the weight of an object depends on \(g\) and decreases slightly with increasing height of the object above the surface of the earth.

The gravitational force \(\vex{F}_g\) on a particle due to the earth depends, like all other forces, only on the relative positions of the interacting objects. Hence the result \(\vex{F}_g = m \vex{g}\) ought to be always true, irrespective of how the particle moves (i.e., irrespective of whether the particle falls downward, or moves along a trajectory like a projectile, or is lying on the floor).

The equation of motion \(m \vex{a} = \vex{F}\) for any particle of mass \(m\) moving under the sole influence of gravity due to the earth can thus always be written as

\[m \vex{a} = m \vex{g}\]

Dividing both sides by \(m\) gives then

\[\vex{a} = \vex{g}\]

Thus we arrive at the result (already used in s \ChRefNo6 and \ChRefNo7) that the acceleration of any particle moving under the sole influence of gravity is always equal to \(\vex{g}\), irrespective of how the particle moves.
