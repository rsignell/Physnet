    A. OVERVIEW

    The information design ideas contained in this handbook can be used to design materials for a variety of instructional delivery modes, from the traditional lecture mode to the newer "self paced" ones. The modules may be used in planning lectures, courses, and examinations. They may supplement textbooks or replace them. In fact, the information design criteria can be used to comparison rate various textbooks as well as instructional materials which compete with them.

    Well-designed information modules can provide instructional support by means of (i) automatic (without instructor intervention) remedial work by the weaker students, relieving the need for lengthy class time reviews; (ii) automatic graduated assistance to students, reducing repetitious office-hour tutoring; 3. insights and points of view which are used by professionals; and 4. material which has had extensive time and revision energy lavished on each module.

    Experimental modes of instructional delivery are currently being developed by scientist-teachers in response to problems they perceive with their courses. These problem-areas include: 1. coping with inadequate and varying student backgrounds and abilities; 2. improving upon students' fragmentary retention of knowledge and skills and poor intuitive understanding of the field; 3. motivating students toward the material itself in service courses; and 4. developing a student record system useful for career planning and for writing letters for recommendation to employers and to professional and graduate schools. Most of the solutions currently being devised to meet these problems require materials with the characteristics of modules.

    The incorporation of flexibility and goal-orientation into materials is one of the basic design features of this handbook. Such materials are expected to allow the rise of new and innovative delivery systems. However, whether specific delivery systems live, die or evolve, a modular information system can continue developing and providing a broad support base for all of them. FullWidthFigure120pt

    a. Limiting the Paragraph Subject Matter

    To enhance storage in the reader's memory, a paragraph should contain a strictly limited number of new information bits. However, it may still express many ideas about these bits. Such bits may be new words, word usages, new phrases, new concepts, etc. Evidence has shown that, to achieve a fixed level of recall, readers

    Memory for Prose, Walter Kintsch, paper presented at the AAAS Meetings, NY, January, 1975. (Preprint, private communication from the author.)require a much greater reading time for a paragraph which has many different bits than they do for a paragraph with only a few oft-used bits, even if both paragraphs contain the same number of statements. In other words, a reader finds it much easier to process complex and extensive information about just a few bits than to process superficial information about many. Thus an efficient paragraph will have limited subject matter, but will explore that subject matter from a number of angles. b. Constructing Paragraph Titles

The Value of Paragraph Titles} There are several ways in which paragraph titles make textual material more effective. First, they can be placed in a Table of Contents, which can then be scanned both to determine or locate content and for purposes of review to test recall after reading. Second, they increase the reader's attention and direct it toward the central points of the paragraph: this reduces reading time \Footnote{Morosky and Willcox, \Quote{Time required to process information as a function of question placement,} {\em Amer. Ed. Res. Jour.}, {\em 7}, 4, 561-7.} and facilitates recall. Most importantly, however, paragraph titles are instrumental in making the organization of textual material apparent to the reader. \Footnote{Rothkopf and Bisbicas, \Quote{Selective facilitation effects of interspersed questions on learning from written material,} {\em Journal of Educational Psychology}, 1967, {\em 58}, 56-61.} As entries in the Table of Contents, they present the reader with an initial framework, or organizational structure, which greatly simplifies comprehension of the textual material. In the text itself, an individual title alerts the reader to the fact that the paragraph fits into a new category and labels that category. \Footnote{Tulving, {\em Organization and Memory}, Academic Press, NY, 1972. For further discussion of the categorization necessary for mental storage and retrieval, see Chapter F, this module.} Interestingly, this \Quote{imposed} textual organization (by paragraph titles) does not seem to hinder readers who prefer to categorize material through use of the topic sentences: such readers, automatically ignore paragraph titles during first reading of such titled material as, for example, in newspapers. {\em d. Must Titles be Placed on Every Paragraph?} It would seem a good policy to insert titles with sufficient frequency so that each title encompasses no more than five to seven new bits of information. \Footnote{This is the limit of the brain's input buffer, its \Quote{short term memory\Quote{. The buffer contents can be categorized by the title, categorization being a condition necessary for storage and retrieval. For a further discussion see Chapter F, this module.} Thus a story with a slow new-bit presentation rate might have a number of paragraphs per title. Scientific material however, seems to fit more naturally into a one-title-per-paragraph format. Although some authors have had to struggle to produce good titles, they have uniformly praised the effect upon the clarity and cohesiveness of their writing. In retrospect, these authors have felt it to be a most valuable aid to designing information. {\em e. The Topic Sentence} Each paragraph should contain a topic sentence which states the main idea and provides a content outline of the paragraph. If the paragraph is well-constructed it will have both limited subject matter and closure around the topic implied by the title, and every sentence in the paragraph will relate to the topic sentence in some fashion. Interestingly, a topic sentence often resembles the conclusion reached via the argument made in the paragraph body. A topic sentence's effect can be greatly enhanced through imaginative use of supportive diagrams and paragraph titles. \Footnote{See Section C.1.e.} Since there are very few paragraphs written simply to pose a questions, the topic sentence is rarely stated in the form of a question. If a question seems at first to be more natural, we suggest actually generating both question and statement forms and then choosing between them. {\em f. Placing the Topic Sentence} The topic sentence should be the first sentence in a paragraph. There are two reasons for this. First, experiments have demonstrated that material is more easily \Footnote{{\em Experimental Studies in Verbal versus Figural Learning and Retention}, Lars Alin; Stockholm, Almquist & Wiskell, 1964.} organized and remembered it if is preceded by a framework in which it can be placed. Since all sentences in a good paragraph relate to the topic sentence, the latter should be placed at the beginning of the paragraph, where it best fulfills this role. Second, the first item or a series is by far the most easily remembered. $^0$ \Footnote{$^0${\em Memory and Attention}, David Norman, John Wiley & Sons, Inc., 1969.} This serial-position effect dictates that the most important sentence, the topic sentence, should come first in a paragraph. 3. Use by Teachers and Professionals} The underlining of words, an the use of exhortations and commands to the reader occur rather frequently in information designed by some teachers, yet are almost totally absent from that designed by professional writers: one can categorize the results in terms of both articulateness and attitude toward the reader. b. Underlining vs Articulation______________________________ The underlining of words usually constitutes an attempt to short cut the information design process. It is certainly quicker and easier to underline a word than it is to give further explanation to the reader, to give examples, to place the word in a broad setting, or to generate images which fully communicate one's strong feelings on a subject. Some authors have found it helpful to generate both the underlined and the fully articulated versions in finished form in order to decide which is better in a particular application. The desire to avoid the articulation effort may bias the results, however, unless the two versions are actually written down and then compared. {\em c. Communicating Respect for the Reader} Materials can be constructed so as to convey a sense of respect for the reader as a conscientious learner. In other words, they can give the reader the impressions of being part of a collective effort, of being an apprentice professional as opposed to being an inferior who is role-bound in a student teacher relationship. One method is to adapt a tone similar to the one you would use in a seminar while communicating your research speciality to colleagues who are not knowledgeable in your field. For example, use of the word \Quote{student} can be replaced by such terms as \Quote{person\Quote{, \Quote{user\Quote{, \Quote{reader\Quote{, \Quote{you\Quote{, and \Quote{one\Quote{. Since the text material is targeted directly toward each individual reader (to facilitate interaction) use of the third person usually seems inappropriate. Likewise, one can avoid producing implicit \Quote{put-downs} via word underlinings, exhortations, authoritative-sounding instructions, and statements about what the reader has already learned or will learn in the future. Statements of the latter type (e.g., \Quote{You have already learned that...}) can be interpreted as implying the antithesis of what they say, conveying a lack of confidence in the ability of the reader to recall prerequisite material. A more directly useful procedure is to simply reference prerequisites and post-options in footnotes $^3$, just as one does in journal articles. \Footnote{$^3$For a discussion of footnote references, see Sect.\,B.4, this handbook.} {\em d. Reasons to Communicate Respect for the Reader} Textual material which demonstrates a respect for the reader improves learning efficiency in two ways. First, it gives the reader a sense of belonging or inclusion in a field of scientific inquiry, a feeling which has been shown to increase interest in the material and thus facilitate learning. $^4$ \Footnote{$^4$Bruner. {\em The Process of Education}. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press (1963).} Second, this technique puts pressure on the reader to interact with the material. Connecting Pattern and Conceptual Memory} Whenever possible, the text can profitably include a simple labeled line drawing (e.g., graph or diagram) which presents equations, concepts, examples, or problems in compact form. Such drawings have the chief benefit of allowing the reader to make use of pattern memory, a highly effective mechanism for the storage of visual patterns. It has been shown that the memory for pictures is superior to the memory for words, since the pictures are encoded both visually, as patterns, and semantically, as concepts. $^6$ \Footnote{$^6$Blesoe. \Quote{Relative Efficacy of Pictures and Words in Eliciting Sense-Impression Responses\Quote{, Perceptual_________{\em and Motor Skills}, 39, (1974) p. 247-51.} When the drawing also contains key concept-word labels, the two memory-traces will be connected by associations $^7$ and the recall of one will entail the recall of the other. \Footnote{$^7$Jenkins, Stock and Deno. \Quote{Children's Recognition and Recall of' Picture and Word Stimuli\Quote{, {\em A.V. Comm. Review}, 17, 3, p. 265-71.} Thus a labeled visual pattern-image has the advantage of providing a single memory entry as an organizational cue for the storage and recall of both types of information. {\em b. Research Supports Simplicity} Psychological research in the areas of perception and memory indicates that an essential principle of effective graphics design is to keep drawings as simple as possible. This criterion of simplicity applies to both the number of elements in the drawing and the patterns in which these elements are arranged. Although one is sometimes tempted to \Quote{flesh out} diagrams with supporting detail, such additional elements will get a share of the reader's attention and memory storage capacity and thus detract from mental processing of the diagram's central theme. Reducing the complexity of patterns is often well worth the author's effort. Interestingly, experimental results indicate that recall errors are a linear function of the amount of information in a picture. $^8$ \Footnote{$^8$Schnore and Partington. \Quote{Immediate Memory for Visual Patterns: Symmetry and Amount of Information\Quote{, {\em Psychonomic Science}, 8 (1967) p. 421-2.} c. Research Supports Symmetry______________________________ Experimental evidence strongly supports the use of symmetry in diagrams. For example, when presented with a choice of either symmetrical or asymmetrical diagrams, subjects prefer the symmetrical alternatives. When paired with similar results showing a preference for simplicity, these results indicate that symmetry tends to simplify complex shapes. $^9^,$ $^0$ \Footnote{$^9$Eisenman and Gellens. \Quote{Preferences for Complexity-Simplicity and Symmetry-Asymmetry\Quote{, {\em Perceptual and Motor Skills}, 26, (1968) p. 88-90.} This makes sense, in that a symmetrical figure can be recalled by use of a redundancy rule, while an asymmetrical figure does not allow such short-cuts. \Footnote{$^0$Eisenman. \Quote{Complexity-Simplicity: 1. Preference for Symmetry and Rejection of Complexity\Quote{, {\em Psychonomic Science}, 8, (1967) p. 169-70.} The benefits of symmetrical diagrams have been even more clearly demonstrated by another experiment in which a direct correlation was found between symmetry and recall. This same study also demonstrated that simplicity was directly related to recall, and that recall was clearly correlated with learner preference. $^1$ \Footnote{$^1$Paraskevopoulus. \Quote{Symmetry: Its Effects on Recall, Preference and Discrimination of Visual Patterns in Relation to Age and Intelligence\Quote{, {\em Dissertation Abstracts}, 28, (1967) p. 1309.} In short, there appears to be a great deal of support for the statement that diagrams should make use of symmetry whenever possible. Recent studies have also indicated that symmetric diagrams are more effective when they are symmetrical about the vertical axis. $^2$ \Footnote{$^2$Deregowski. \Quote{Symmetry, Gestalt, and Information Theory\Quote{, {\em Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology}, 23, (1971) p. 381-5.} {\em d. The omission of such local-adaptation information appears to greatly diminish transportability. We suggest that this type of information go in a Local Guide which accompanies your module. General Aspects of Transportability: Modularity} The most important technical design characteristics which enhance transportability appear to be the ease of content perception and the ease of adaptation for local conditions. In other words, the designer interested in transportability should make it easy to see what skills and knowledge the module can develop, given various sets of input skills and knowledge. A module which has been well-designed in this regard can be described as modular, since it can be used in a variety of situations. Note however, that the current state of the art in instructional information design is that of after-the-fact modularity: most authors complete a module before adding the documentation for modularity. Quite possibly, future info-designers will actually design modularity into their material, with a resulting enhancement of adaptability and transportability. {\em 2. Nature of Prerequisites Statements} A module's statements of Input Skills usefully delineate those skills which the author assumes the reader already possesses. Input Skills statements free the author from having to develop those skills in the module itself, as well as inform potential users of necessary preparation. Thus the statements should be in a form the author would find useful in testing readers' adequacy of preparation. If a particular prerequisite skill is developed in a module already in the system, that module can be referenced in parentheses immediately after the statement itself. Judging Which Input Skills to List_____________________________________ For deciding which Input Skills to list, a good rule of thumb is that no item in the list is one which would normally be considered prerequisite to another item in the same list. A prerequisite to a prerequisite is not usually listed. In other words, one normally lists only those prerequisites which produce the module's direct incoming links on a prerequisite network diagram. $^5$ \Footnote{$^5$See \Quote{Constructing a Prerequisite Network Diagram\Quote{, Sect.\,H.5, this module.} {\em d. Reasons to State Input Skills} Statements of Input Skills can serve these purposes: \begin{itemize} \item [1.] Help the module author decided which skills are to be developed in the module vs which skills are to be assumed. \item [2.] Assist the reader prepare adequately before starting into the module. \item [3.] Aid teachers in determining the appropriateness of the module for certain students or classes, or for specific time-points in classes. \item [4.] Help users to effectively sequence modules. \item [5.] Assist a system manager locate gaps and so determine needs for new modules. \end{itemize} Input Skill statements have been found to be an absolute necessity for transportability from one institution to another. Without such statements, a potential user must determine the needed background by carefully going through every sentence and every problem in the module. Few teachers have such time available. The author, being intimately familiar with every facet of the module, can save us from having to repeat such analyses at each of our own sites by simply stating the module's needed and advised entry skills. Furthermore, authors who are teachers usually find that such statements allow the weaker students in their own classes to properly prepare for class periods, thus helping to alleviate the need for time absorbing reviews and office-hour tutoring. {\em 4. General Guidelines} Skills which the reader can acquire from the module are most easily and accurately delineated by the author, since anyone else would have to first become thoroughly familiar with all aspects of the module. The statements of Output Skills need to be sufficiently complete and specific so that: 1. the module can be evaluated on the basis intended by the author. 2. potential users can determine whether the module fits their needs. 3. users are assisted in evaluating individual learning from the module. 4. the module's Output Skills can be compared to the Input Skills listed for other modules in order to identify post-options. 5. the system manager and authors can more easily locate gaps between the modules and their potential post options. b{\em . This particular set of labels has the advantage of clear communication and ease of use. With this system, the label to apply to any given skill is easily determined from the examination method used for determining that skill's attainment. Other, more complex, labeling systems have been used elsewhere. $^5$ \Footnote{$^5$For example: {\em Learning Objectives for Individualized Instruction}: {\em Science}, Westinghouse Learning Press, Sunnyvale, CA (1975).} {\em c. Project Skills_________________ A Project Skill is comprehensive one, often combining skills in the areas of knowledge, rule-application, problem-solving, analysis, synthesis, judgement, group effort, and communication. Lumping the Knowledge Skills} Some authors dislike listing many small Knowledge Skills, and there are several ways of dealing with the problem. One is to simply not write them, assuming that they are of little value as skills. Another is to replace them, as feasible, with: 1. an Index $^7$; plus \Footnote{$^7$See Sect.\,B.5, this module.} 2. an explicit or implicit problem-solving skills, \Quote{Apply strategies for understanding and problem-solving at all appropriate points in this module.} $^8$ \Footnote{$^8$See Sect.\,E, this module, for a listing of the strategies, along with a discussion of their use, and references to the literature.} A third approach is to combine them into one or two comprehensive Knowledge Skills, such as: \Quote{Prepare and deliver a complete lecture on how to solve ...\Quote{; or \Quote{State the steps to be followed in solving ...\Quote{. Purposes of a Model Exam} A Model Exam is sometimes added to a module's Local Guide so that learners can gauge the thoroughness of their study. It may also serve as a guide for teachers wishing to construct actual exams based upon the skills developed in the unit. Since most real exams test a wider range of skills than those found in a single module, the model exam is somewhat restrictive. For this reason, and because it is usually non-modular, we suggest packaging it separate from the rest of the module. {\em b. OVERVIEW} One of the greatest assets of the print medium is its ability to evolve through critiquing, reflective analysis, and revision. $^9$ \Footnote{$^9$For a further discussion of the ability of written communication to evolve, see David Riesman, \Quote{Oral and Written Traditions\Quote{, {\em Explorations in Communication}, Ed. by Carpenter and McLuhan, Boston: Beacon Press (1960).} Jean-Paul Sartre said that one of the worst results of his late-life blindness was a drastically reduced capability for the all important revision phase of composition. Reading his words back to him was insufficient: he had to see them to properly analyze and revise them. Although almost all of us must also hand craft our units, our efficiency increases dramatically with our repertoire of design skills. A crucial element in developing those skills is obtaining detailed feedback from the information's consumers. This section contains methods which authors have found useful in obtaining such feedback. {\em 2. CONSULTING} CONTENTS a. Silent Consulting . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . b. Why Not Oral Consulting? . . . . . . . . . . . c. Dealing with Hostile Reactions . . . . . . . . {\em a. Silent Consulting} The most potent method we have found for debugging modules is that of person-to-person silent consulting. This means that, when a learner comes to us for assistance, we give no oral reply. Instead, we alter the learner's module copy in such a way as to relieve the learner's problem. This may involve changing an answer, inserting lines of text, labeling a diagram, or writing a new sequence for the Special Assistance Supplement. For this revision we frequently make use of \Quote{Liquid Paper} or similar products. If the learner says that the new version still doesn't work, we probe for the cause and revise again. When the learner is satisfied that the roadblock has been removed, we make a copy for the next printing of the module. One of the obvious advantages of modules' small size is the ease with which they can be revised and reprinted, and thus can evolve. {\em b. Why Not Oral Consulting?} We far prefer silent over oral consulting for debugging modules because: 1. it more easily allows the learner to reflect and decide whether the proposed \Quote{fix} really works; and 2. a permanent record is generated of that which actually worked. We might even call oral consulting an intellectual tragedy, since its emotional impact can easily lead one into a premature judgement that the learner's problem has been sufficiently solved. }
